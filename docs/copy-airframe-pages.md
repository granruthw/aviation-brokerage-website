## Page 1 — Insuring a Cirrus SR22: what actually drives your renewal

*Target query cluster: "cirrus sr22 insurance", "sr22 insurance requirements", "cirrus insurance training requirements"*

The SR22 is the best-selling piston single of the 21st century, and its insurance market is correspondingly deep: most carriers that write owner-flown aircraft will quote a well-presented SR22 risk. That does not mean every SR22 owner sees the same outcome. Underwriters look past the airframe to the pilot, the training record, and the mission — and the spread between a well-documented application and a thin one can be substantial.

**What underwriters weigh most.** Time-in-type sits at the top of the list. The SR22 is fast, slippery, and unforgiving of unstabilized approaches, and the claims record of low-time-in-type transitions shaped how the whole fleet is priced. A pilot stepping up from a Skyhawk with 40 hours of SR22 time is a different risk — in the underwriter's eyes — from a 1,500-hour owner with 800 in type, even if their total time is similar. Instrument rating and actual instrument currency matter for the same reason: the SR22 is bought to travel, and traveling airplanes meet weather.

**Training is the lever you control.** Cirrus built a training culture around the airframe — factory-standardized transition and recurrent programs and the Cirrus-standardized instructor network (CSIP) — and underwriters reward engagement with it. Completing a recognized transition course at purchase, and recurrent training on a regular cycle [VERIFY specific carrier training requirements at quote time], is among the strongest signals an application can carry. Many carriers make initial and recurrent training an explicit policy condition for the SR22; read those conditions carefully, because a lapsed training requirement can affect coverage when it matters most.

**CAPS changes the conversation — carefully.** The Cirrus Airframe Parachute System is a real safety system with a real record of saves, and the fleet's fatal-accident trend improved markedly as training emphasis shifted toward CAPS deployment decision-making [VERIFY current fleet statistics before publish]. From an insurance perspective, though, the parachute cuts both ways: a deployment that saves the occupants typically results in a hull loss. That is exactly the trade the system is designed to make — but it means hull rates on the SR22 reflect deployment economics, not just crash economics. Owners should insure hull value realistically: an agreed value set too low invites a painful gap at the worst time, and one set too high invites premium waste.

**Hull values and the market.** SR22 hull values run a wide range — from earlier G1/G2 airframes to late-model G6/G7 aircraft well north of a million dollars [VERIFY current market ranges] — and the hull premium is usually the dominant share of the total. Liability structure is the other pillar: many owners carry $1M limits with per-passenger sublimits, while some missions and lenders push toward smooth (non-sublimited) limits, which are harder to place and priced accordingly. Understanding the difference before you're asked to choose is worth an hour of anyone's time — it is the single most consequential checkbox on the application.

**What a well-presented SR22 application looks like.** Complete pilot history with time-in-type broken out; documented transition and recurrent training with dates and providers; instrument currency; hangar status; a realistic agreed hull value; and a clean narrative of mission (where you fly, how often, what for). Owners who present their flying honestly and completely are consistently better served by the market than owners who submit the minimum.

## Page 2 — Insuring a Beechcraft Bonanza: the retract question, answered honestly

*Target query cluster: "bonanza insurance cost", "beechcraft bonanza insurance", "v-tail bonanza insurance"*

The Bonanza has been in continuous production since 1947, which makes its fleet — and its insurance market — unlike anything else in general aviation. A 1955 V-tail and a late-model G36 are, to an underwriter, nearly different aircraft: different values, different systems, different pilot populations. What they share is retractable gear, strong type-club culture, and a market that has been pricing them for three-quarters of a century.

**Retractable gear is the first filter.** Gear-up landings are among the most common claims in the retract fleet — expensive, rarely injurious, and heavily weighted in pricing. Underwriters respond by caring about retract time specifically: total time matters, but time in retractable-gear aircraft, and time in the Bonanza itself, matter more. Transitioning pilots should expect training requirements as policy conditions [VERIFY per carrier at quote time], and experienced Bonanza pilots with long claim-free retract histories are the fleet's preferred risk.

**The type club is an underwriting asset.** The American Bonanza Society's Beechcraft Pilot Proficiency Program (BPPP) has trained Bonanza pilots for decades and is well known to every aviation underwriter [VERIFY current program name and format]. Documented, recurring participation in type-specific proficiency training is one of the clearest ways a Bonanza owner strengthens an application — it signals exactly the discipline the loss history says matters. If you fly a Bonanza and haven't engaged with the type club's training, you are leaving both safety and negotiating position on the table.

**Age, value, and the airframe spectrum.** Insuring a $60,000 1960s airframe and a $900,000 G36 are different problems [VERIFY value ranges]. Older airframes raise parts-availability and repair-cost questions that push the hull premium as a percentage of value higher than owners expect; late-model airframes carry higher absolute values with correspondingly larger hull premiums. The V-tail fleet adds its own history: decades of ruddervator lore, an airworthiness record that was ultimately addressed [VERIFY], and an owner community that knows the airplane deeply. Present the airframe's maintenance story — recent annuals, known-AD compliance, who maintains it — as part of the application; on older retracts it genuinely moves outcomes.

**Liability structure deserves real thought.** Bonanzas are six-seat travelers in their later configurations, and the per-passenger sublimit question is sharper when the back seats are full of family. Smooth limits exist in this market for well-qualified pilots but are priced meaningfully higher and demand a stronger pilot resume. Whatever you choose, choose it knowingly — the difference between $1M smooth and $1M with $100K per-passenger sublimits is the difference between two very different protections wearing the same headline number.

**The honest summary.** The Bonanza market rewards exactly what the type rewards: currency, training, and respect for the gear handle. A documented BPPP habit, broken-out retract and type time, and a maintenance narrative worthy of the airframe put an owner in the strongest position the market offers.

## Page 3 — Insuring a Mooney: precision airplane, precision application

*Target query cluster: "mooney insurance", "mooney m20 insurance cost", "mooney ovation insurance"*

Mooneys occupy a distinctive corner of the high-performance single market: the most efficient way ever devised to turn avgas into cross-country speed, flown by owners who tend to be engineers at heart. The insurance market sees the type through two lenses at once — a retract with a long fleet history, and a pilot population that skews toward precision. Your application decides which lens the underwriter reaches for.

**The gear, again — with a Mooney twist.** Like every retract, the M20 fleet's claims history is shaped by gear-up landings, and retract time and time-in-type are the first questions on any Mooney application. The Mooney adds its own consideration: the airframe sits low, approaches are flown on tight speed tolerances, and the type is famously intolerant of carrying extra knots into the flare. Underwriters know that a pilot who is truly current in a Mooney is a disciplined pilot almost by definition — and that a rusty one is carrying more risk than his logbook totals suggest. **Training culture counts here too.** The Mooney community's proficiency programs [VERIFY current MAPA PPP program status and name] serve the same underwriting function BPPP serves for the Bonanza fleet: documented, type-specific recurrent training that tells the market you take the airplane seriously. **What strengthens a Mooney application.** Broken-out time in type and in retracts; documented type-specific training with dates; instrument currency; hangar status (the fleet's age makes weather exposure a real hull consideration); a maintenance narrative naming who knows the airframe; and an agreed value grounded in the current market rather than a decade-old memory. The Mooney rewards pilots who fly the numbers. So does its insurance market.
